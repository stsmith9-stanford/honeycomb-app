Round 2 · daily-modes references · don't over-anchor on the hexagon

Where does the hex earn its keep?

The product is named Honeycomb, so the hex is the brand-defining shape. But the temptation is to use it everywhere — as progress dots, mode icons, source thumbnails, zone badges, prompt frames. Looking at how the most-installed daily-habit apps actually solve these problems, almost none of them rely on a single load-bearing shape. They use color, typography, and brand register to carry identity, and let conventional primitives (rings, soft rounded rectangles, illustrations) do the daily-use work.

Below: a side-by-side comparison of where the current daily-mode mockups lean on the hex, where they shouldn't, and what the top-tier references show instead.

The TL;DR before the comparisons.

If you only have 30 seconds:

Keep the hex for

  • The Blend cell visualization (the 1+3 zones) — load-bearing
  • The Reflect icon on the home screen — small composition of 4 hexes acting as the brand mark
  • The brand mark / logo / app icon — outside the product surface
  • The wordmark "Honeycomb" stays as-is

Drop the hex from

  • Mode icons on the home screen — use simple emoji / illustration instead
  • Read mode progress dots — use Apple-Fitness-style ring strip
  • Source glyph containers in Read mode — use plain rounded thumbnails
  • Zone tag inner shapes — keep the gradient color, drop the inner hex
  • Connect mode "today's proposal" framing — use a Bumble/Headspace single-CTA card
  • Vault-review mini-cell — keep this one (it's encoding the actual zone structure)
Where the hex earns its keep

Two surfaces, not seven.

The hex's job is to encode set-theoretic structure — the four zones of triadic overlap. That work happens in exactly two places. Everywhere else, the hex is decoration competing for the user's attention with the actual content.

1. The Blend cell visualization

The honeycomb of 1 + 3 hexes encodes the three-way intersection and three pairwise-only zones. The geometry is the data. This is the brand-defining moment and the screenshot.

2. The Reflect icon on home

A miniature 4-hex composition acts as a navigational thumbnail back to the Blend page. Like Apple Fitness's tiny activity ring icon — it points to the place where the cell visualization lives.

2.5. The Vault-review mini-cell (borderline)

Each item shows a mini 1+3 cell as its destination picker — this is encoding actual zone structure, so it earns its hex. Keep this one.

3. The brand mark itself

Logo, app icon, splash screen, marketing — the hex carries identity off-product. That's where it lives, not inside the daily-use surface.

Swap 02 · Read mode

Progress strip: hex dots → Apple-Fitness-style ring strip.

Daily-progress dots already have a canonical form. It's a ring, not a hex.

Now in the mockup

Seven small hex dots at the bottom of the Read mode card. Filled ones = done, current one is larger and glowing, empty ones = ahead.

The shape is invented for the sake of the brand. There's a 25-year-old convention that already does this job.

→ See 07-mockup-read-mode.html
Apple Fitness
Horizontal weekday strip (M T W T F S S) with activity rings underneath. The current day is bigger and brighter.
Apple Fitness
Same strip variant. Activity rings carry the "did you do it today" question with no extra UI.
Matter
"Everything in moderation! A new selection will be ready tomorrow." Bounded session done in pure typography.
What changes. Replace the seven hex dots with seven small filled-ring icons on a horizontal day-strip. The Apple Fitness ring is one of the most instantly readable progress primitives in consumer software. It also connects to the Blend metaphor better than a hex — concentric rings = sets, which is what overlap actually is. Save a "you're done today" copy line for the end (Matter does this well).

Swap 04 · Connect mode

Proposal card: dense info-block → soft brand-washed card.

Bumble's question card is the canonical "one big prompt, one big action" pattern. Honeycomb's proposal can borrow the shape and keep its own copy.

Now in the mockup

Three "Why now / Why this / Why now" rows in a dense reasoning block; AI draft preview in a dashed box; three-button action row.

Information-dense but emotionally cold. The proposal feels like an audit report, not an invitation.

→ See 08-mockup-connect-mode.html
Bumble
One big yellow rounded-rectangle card. One question. One "Send question" button. The card stack hints at the rhythm.
Peanut
White card on neutral background. Small "Ask me about..." label, big bold question, big red Answer button.
Headspace
Single Check-in CTA. Doesn't show all the reasoning — invites you in first, surfaces detail after.
What changes. Collapse the "Why" reasoning block from three always-visible rows into a single expandable line: "Why this connection? ▾". Headline becomes one assertive line — "Coffee with Humzah, Thursday 4pm" — in big serif on a softly tinted card. Send becomes the dominant action; reasoning is one tap away. The current mockup over-justifies; the goal is to invite, not to argue.
Swap 05 · All views

Zone tag: hex inner glyph → color-only pill with avatar pips.

The avatar pips already say "which two people." The inner hex is teaching the same lesson twice.

Now in the mockup

Every card carries a "zone tag" pill that contains: a small hex glyph, the label ("S × H · just you two"), and an avatar-pip stack.

The hex glyph is redundant with the pips. Reducing it to a colored pill keeps the structural information without doubling up.

Swap 06 · Read mode interaction

Card-swipe affordance: missing → visible card stack + swipe glyph.

If we're inheriting the Readwise swipe pattern, the swipe affordance should be obvious from screen one.

Now in the mockup

Read mode currently shows one card with a "Next" button. It's stateful, not gestural.

The team's reference was "the Readwise daily review swipe that becomes a habit." The current mockup doesn't yet show that it's swipeable.

→ See 07-mockup-read-mode.html

Speak
Stacked review-cards depth-cue. Three cards visible at slight angles makes the deck legible.
Bumble
Same pattern. The next card peeks out from behind the current one. You know there's a deck.
What changes. Add a visible card stack: 2-3 cards peeking out from behind the active one, slightly offset and dimmed. Keep the explicit "Save / Send / Next" buttons (the swipe is the gesture; the buttons are the keyboard shortcut for hesitant users). Optionally show a one-time swipe-tutorial overlay on first run — Google Photos and Tinder both do this, and users get it instantly. The progress strip from Swap 02 then tells you how deep the deck goes.

The net effect. The hex stops being a tile pattern and starts being a brand mark. It shows up twice in the daily-use surface (the Reflect icon, the Blend cell) and otherwise gets out of the way. The product still reads as Honeycomb because the color register, the editorial typography, and the per-zone color logic stay intact. We don't lose the brand — we earn it back by using the hex sparingly.
