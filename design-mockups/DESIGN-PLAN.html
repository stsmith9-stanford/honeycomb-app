Honeycomb · M2 design plan · locked

The product is named for its shape.
Now the shape is the product.

After a 400-screen Mobbin sweep, three Blend mockups in distinct directions, and a side-by-side spec page comparing all four views — the design lands on one direction: a honeycomb cell where the center hex is what all three of you share, and three satellite hexes are what each pair shares without the third.

Same primitive scales across Blend, Vault review, Shared shelf, and Conversations. Every approval, every save, every prompt has a zone in the cell. That structural consistency is the design — not a coat of paint on top of one.

DESIGN PLAN · MAY 12, 2026 · SHAWN · YEDU · HUMZAH
The decision · locked

"Direction B — the honeycomb cell + cream editorial body — is the visual system for M2."

Direction A (cream editorial pair) was the safest but most forgettable. Direction C (dark constellation) was the most ambitious but tilts toward astrology-app energy in copy. Direction B is the only one with a brand-native metaphor, native triadic geometry, and a clear extension story across all four product surfaces.

01 · the argument

Four reasons this is the right direction.

Argument 01

The product is literally named "Honeycomb." The hex cell is the brand.

This is the only direction where the name and the visual are the same idea. We don't have to explain what a honeycomb is — every screen renders it. A new user opening the app for the first time can read the geometry of their relationships without us writing copy that translates the metaphor.

The closest reference (NYT Spelling Bee's 6-around-1) proves the cell pattern reads instantly even with no UI training.

Argument 02

Triadic geometry encoded as zones, not chart geometry.

Set-theoretically a triadic overlap has exactly four zones: one three-way intersection and three pairwise-only zones. The honeycomb cell of 1 + 3 maps onto those four zones perfectly. The amber center = all three. Each satellite = one pair without the third.

Compare to the chart alternatives we considered: How We Feel's translucent Venn (works but reads as math), Feeld's constellation (works but reads as astrology), or any percentage / VS framing (reads as a leaderboard, conflicts with mutual exchange).

Argument 03

One primitive, four surfaces — the visual scales.

The cell isn't just the Blend visualization. It's the routing destination in Vault review (approve into a zone). It's the provenance badge on every shared-shelf save (a small zone hex tells you which two/three contributed). It's the anchor in Conversations (the question lives in a center-hex frame; three peer answer cards live below).

That structural consistency is what makes Honeycomb feel like a product instead of four views in a folder.

Argument 04

Cream editorial body gives the knowledge content its register.

The hex is the brand-defining moment, but most of the actual reading happens in long editorial cards: highlighted quotes, prompt questions, dated journal entries. The hex carries the identity; the Tolan-style cream serif typography carries the content.

Direction A had this register without the hex. Direction B keeps the register and adds the visual mnemonic. It's strictly more product per square pixel.

The honest tradeoff. The cell composition (1 center + 3 satellites) is hex-natural but not the platonic 6-around-1 Spelling Bee shape. It's a "triadic cell" — a sub-cell, three of six satellites. This is geometrically correct (the missing three satellites would be where the user avatars sit at the three outer vertices), but it does mean the product will need a small piece of onboarding the first time a user sees it: "the three of you sit at the corners, and the four zones in the middle are what's shared."

View 02 of 4 · Vault review

Approve into a zone, not just yes/no.

Open full mockup ↗

The agent surfaces private vault items. Each item shows a mini honeycomb cell on the right — four destination hexes. Tapping a hex approves the item and places it in that zone (center for all three, satellite for a specific pair). The agent suggests a zone; you confirm or override. Each item also reveals its reasoning — what made the agent flag it — so trust is built before approval.

Below: a filterable grid where every saved item carries a small hex chip showing which zone it lives in (center / S×Y / Y×H / S×H). Reactions show contributor avatars instead of generic counts.

View 04 of 4 · Conversations

One prompt, three peer answers.

Open full mockup ↗

The current prompt sits in a center-hex amber frame. Three answer cards (one per member) sit below, each tagged with the member's accent color. An AI-drafted 1:1 text sits below, with tone-adjust controls (the draft is clearly "not sent yet"). Past prompts use Tiimo-style overlapping avatar pips to show who answered each. 03 · how the cell scales

One primitive. Four roles. No paint.

View
Role of the hex
What it solves
Blend
The whole canvas. A single cell of 1+3 hexes representing the four zones of triadic overlap.
Makes set-theoretic structure (3-way ∩ 3 × pairwise) visible without resorting to a math diagram.
Vault review
The routing destination. A miniature cell shown on each agent-surfaced item; tap a hex = approve + place.
Collapses two decisions (approve? where?) into one gesture. Encodes per-recipient privacy structurally, not as a separate setting.
Shared shelf
The provenance chip. A tiny zone-hex sits at the top of every save card, indicating which zone it belongs to.
Lets users sort and filter their shelf by relationship structure — "show only what S × Y is reading" — without verbal labels.
Conversations
The prompt anchor. Each prompt lives in an amber center-hex frame; answers cluster below as peer cards.
Makes group prompts feel different from 1:1 prompts structurally (3-card cluster vs. 2-card cluster), not just by typography.
04 · risks and mitigations

What could go wrong, and the answer.

The cell could read as "puzzle" instead of "knowledge."

Risk: The NYT-Spelling-Bee reference is so strong that users might first read the cell as a game tile.

Mitigation: The editorial serif typography and the cream paper register do most of the lifting against this. The hex hero is amber; everything around it is editorial. First-run onboarding uses one sentence: "the three of you sit at the corners, the four zones in the middle are what you share."

"Just two of you" satellite zones could feel like second-class citizens.

Risk: If the amber center hex dominates the page, the three pairwise hexes might read as afterthoughts. That would silently demote the relationships between subsets of the triad.

Mitigation: Treat the four zones as equal in kind, different in density. Same hex shape, same border treatment; only the amber fill of the center distinguishes the all-three zone. Pairwise zones can be the dominant content for any pair where the third member hasn't engaged a theme yet.

Doesn't scale beyond N=3.

Risk: The 1+3 cell only works for triads. A four-person blend would need a different geometry (1+3 pairwise becomes 1+6 pairwise plus 1+4 three-ways).

Mitigation: This is by design for M2. Honeycomb's scoped product is pair + triad. N=4+ is a v2 design problem — and the cell does extend cleanly to 4 (1 center + 6 satellites = the platonic Spelling Bee shape, with 4 three-way zones + 6 pairwise) without changing the visual primitive.

The hex shape is harder to lay out at small screen sizes.

Risk: Hexagons require more vertical space than rectangles for the same content. On mobile, the cell might dominate the screen.

Mitigation: Mobile breakpoint reduces hex size proportionally and tightens the trifold-column body into a stack. The cell stays above the fold on mobile; the editorial body scrolls below. Tested at 760px width in current mockups; smaller breakpoints to follow.
